# Koblenz–Wadsworth Twin HED Falls: Astrophysics Modeling Dossier

**TL;DR:** Two HED achondrite meteorites — Koblenz (brecciated eucrite, Germany, 8 Mar 2026) and Wadsworth (monomict eucrite, Ohio, 17 Mar 2026) — fell just **9 days and 98.2° apart**. Both originated from the crust of a differentiated asteroid, most likely **4 Vesta** or a Vesta-family body. This document compiles every known physical, dynamical, and compositional parameter for both events to serve as input data for orbital mechanics, ablation modeling, fragmentation analysis, and parent body disruption simulations. Critical open question: **cosmic ray exposure age analysis** (underway at Felsenkeller Laboratory and WWU Münster) will determine whether they share a common CRE age — the smoking gun for a recent parent body disruption event.

| Parameter | Value | Source |

The Koblenz trajectory has been computed from **multiple independent datasets**: witness reports submitted to the International Meteor Organization (IMO), the Geophysical Institute (GPI) at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), and the Luxembourg National Seismic Network (LuxSNet). The LuxSNet analysis used **34 seismic stations** in Luxembourg and surrounding regions, mapping signal arrival times from 30 to 440 seconds after the event onset at 17:56 UTC[^73^]. The KIT-GPI analysis produced a trajectory map showing both the IMO-computed path (blue) and the KIT-GPI seismic-derived path (orange), which appear broadly consistent[^73^]. The AMS radiant (RA 33°, Dec −11°) places this event in a region of the sky distinct from the Anthelion source — it is instead consistent with a **low-inclination, possibly Apollo-type or Aten-type orbit**.

The absence of instrumental velocity measurement is a significant data gap. The **European AllSky7 fireball network** recorded the event[^30^], and the data should in principle yield a trajectory solution with velocity via multi-station triangulation. The AMS Q1 report notes that radiants computed from witness-based trajectory fits carry typical uncertainties of **10–20 degrees**[^9^].

The classification evolution is noteworthy: the AMS Q1 report (25 March 2026) initially listed Koblenz as **suspected diogenite**[^9^], which was corrected on 31 March to **brecciated eucrite** following WWU Münster's thin-section analysis announced on 2 April[^73^]. Both are HED achondrites, so the parent body type (differentiated asteroid, likely Vesta) remained unchanged. The distinction between brecciated eucrite and diogenite is petrographically significant: eucrites are basaltic crustal rocks, while diogenites are deeper-seated orthopyroxenites. The brecciated texture indicates **impact fragmentation and re-lithification** on the parent body.

### 1.5 Cosmogenic Radionuclide Analysis

On **22 March 2026**, six small fragments weighing ~2.5 g total were delivered to the **Felsenkeller Laboratory** of VKTA (Strahlenschutz, Analytik & Entsorgung Rossendorf) in Dresden for measurement of **cosmogenic short-lived radionuclides**[^73^]. The Felsenkeller facility is a unique underground laboratory located in a former brewery, covered by **45 metres of rock** that shields cosmic radiation — essential for detecting the weak gamma emission from cosmogenic nuclides[^93^]. The analysis aims to determine:

- **Cosmic ray exposure (CRE) age**: how long the meteoroid was exposed to cosmic rays in space
- **Terrestrial age**: how long ago the meteorite fell (confirming the witnessed fall date)
- **Pre-atmospheric size**: from the depth-dependent nuclide production profile

These data are critical for testing the **common parent body disruption hypothesis**. If Koblenz and Wadsworth share a similar CRE age (~10–50 Ma is typical for HEDs), that would support a recent collisional origin. If the CRE ages differ significantly, they likely represent independent delivery from the Vesta meteoroid complex. As of the last Karmaka update (11 April 2026), results were pending[^73^].

The Wadsworth radiant at **RA 112°, Dec +77°** places it at very high northern declination — distinct from both the Koblenz radiant and the Anthelion source. This high-declination position is consistent with the **"High Declination" cluster** identified in the AMS Q1 analysis, where 12 events were recorded in Q1 2026 compared to 0–5 in prior years[^9^]. The **98.2° separation** between the Koblenz and Wadsworth radiants is the key angular parameter for parent body modeling: if both originated from the same disruption event, the angular separation constrains the geometry of ejecta dispersal.

### 2.3 Instrumental Detection and Multi-Sensor Data

Wadsworth is one of the most thoroughly instrumented meteorite falls in recent history. The following sensors captured the event:

| Sensor System | Data Captured | Source |
|---------------|---------------|--------|
| **GOES-19 GLM** | Near-infrared optical detection; fireball light curve | Karmaka[^46^] |
| **KCLE (Cleveland radar)** | Doppler signature of falling fragments | Wikipedia[^44^] |
| **TLVE (Cleveland Airport TDWR)** | Terminal Doppler radar; fragment tracking | Wikipedia[^44^] |
| **KPBZ (Pittsburgh radar)** | Doppler signature of falling fragments | Wikipedia[^44^] |
| **OhioSeis seismic network** | Sonic boom detection at 4+ stations; trajectory reconstruction | Karmaka[^46^] |
| **Dashcam/security cameras** | Video from I-80 PA, Youngstown OH, Stow OH, Elyria OH, etc. | Karmaka[^46^] |

The **GOES-19 GLM detection** is particularly valuable: the Geostationary Lightning Mapper is a near-infrared optical detector that captured the fireball at **13:01 UTC** (9:01 AM ET), providing a calibrated light curve and timing independent of ground-based observations[^46^]. The **three-radar correlation** (KCLE, TLVE, KPBZ) enabled NASA's Meteoroid Environment Office to compute the strewn field with high precision, predicting where fragments of different masses would land[^44^]. The composite radar image shows **blue-gray pixels representing falling meteorite signatures** over a 12-minute period[^46^]. The OhioSeis seismic network recorded the sonic boom at stations in Chesterland, Chagrin River Park, Lake Erie Bluffs, and Red Brook Metropark, with arrival times varying by distance from the trajectory[^46^].

The Wadsworth meteorites are described as having **"dark-black glossy fusion crust"** with significant variation in crust appearance[^45^]. The 11.1-g specimen found by Gabe Leidy is notable as an **oriented meteorite** — one that maintained stable atmospheric flight orientation, producing a characteristic shape with a rounded front and textured rear[^46^]. The 509.9-g mass found by Mark Sokol was **almost fully embedded in soft soil** when discovered, indicating it reached terminal velocity before impact[^46^].

The **~84 kg potential mass** is an intriguing radar signature that, if confirmed, would make Wadsworth one of the largest HED meteorite falls on record. The signature was detected in the radar data south of the main strewn field, but no searchers have yet reported finding it[^46^].

### 3.2 The 98.2° Radiant Separation

The **98.2° angular separation** between the Koblenz and Wadsworth radiants is the most important constraint for parent body disruption models[^9^]. For two meteoroids to arrive at Earth from the same disruption event with this angular separation, several scenarios are possible:

**Scenario A: Isotropic ejecta from a disruption near the parent body's perihelion.** If a collision or rotational breakup occurred near the asteroid's closest approach to the Sun, ejecta could be distributed across a wide range of orbital parameters. Over time, secular perturbations (particularly from Jupiter and Venus) would disperse the debris. The 98° separation could reflect this natural dispersion.

**Scenario B: Two separate disruption events on the same parent body.** The different petrographic types (brecciated eucrite vs. monomict eucrite) suggest different source depths or locations on the parent body. A major collision could have ejected material from multiple crustal layers simultaneously, with the angular separation reflecting the ejecta velocity field.

**Scenario C: Independent delivery from the Vesta meteoroid complex.** HED meteorites are delivered to Earth through a well-understood dynamical pathway: the **ν6 secular resonance** and **3:1 mean-motion resonance with Jupiter** transport material from the Vesta family in the main belt to Earth-crossing orbits. Two independent meteoroids could be delivered through this pathway within days of each other purely by chance — though the statistical probability is low.

The **cosmic ray exposure age** is the discriminant: if both falls have CRE ages within ~1–2 Ma of each other, Scenario A or B is strongly favored. If the CRE ages differ by >5 Ma, Scenario C becomes more likely.

### 3.3 HED Meteorite Fall Statistics

HED achondrites are among the rarest meteorite falls. Of the ~1,100 witnessed falls in the Meteoritical Bulletin database, **eucrites constitute only ~1.5%** and **diogenites ~0.5%** of all falls. The simultaneous occurrence of **two confirmed HED falls within 9 days** is extraordinary. For context:

| Event | Date | Type | Location | Time since previous HED fall |
|-------|------|------|----------|------------------------------|
| Serra de Magé | 19 Jun 2023 | Eucrite | Brazil | — |
| Wadsworth | 17 Mar 2026 | Monomict eucrite | Ohio, USA | ~2.7 years |
| Koblenz | 8 Mar 2026 | Brecciated eucrite | Germany | **9 days before Wadsworth** |

The last US eucrite fall before Wadsworth was in **1933** — a gap of **93 years**[^48^]. The statistical improbability of two HED falls within 9 days, from different radiants, is a strong motivation for the common disruption hypothesis.

### 4.2 Modeling Approaches Suggested by Available Data

Given the data available, the following modeling approaches are feasible:

**Orbital mechanics (Wadsworth only, for now).** With entry velocity (17.5 km/s), entry altitude (80.6 km), radiant (RA 112°, Dec +77°), and fall time (12:57 UTC, 17 Mar 2026), a heliocentric orbit can be computed using standard meteor orbit determination software (e.g., the **Western Meteor Python Library**, or the **Fireball Processing Tool**). This orbit can then be integrated backward to test for proximity to the Vesta family and to the ν6 and 3:1 resonances. For Koblenz, the lack of instrumental velocity is a major limitation, though an approximate orbit could be estimated from the radiant and assumed moderate velocity (~15–20 km/s).

**Ablation and fragmentation modeling (both).** The NASA ARES **Jormungandr2 dark flight model** has been successfully applied to similar events (e.g., the Cape Cod Bay fall of 30 May 2026)[^99^]. For Wadsworth, the known entry parameters (velocity, altitude, mass, fragmentation altitude) provide excellent input. For Koblenz, the airburst altitude (~50 km) and "few metres" size can be used with estimated velocity to model the luminous flight and fragmentation sequence. The **DFMC (Dark Flight Monte Carlo)** code developed by Moilanen & Gritsevich[^98^] is well-suited for this analysis.

**Parent body disruption modeling.** If the CRE ages match, the 98.2° radiant separation and 9-day arrival time difference constrain the ejecta velocity field and the orbital evolution time. The **ejecta velocity** required to produce 98° of radiant separation after orbital evolution can be estimated. For a Vesta-family source, typical ejecta velocities from cratering impacts are **100–500 m/s**, while catastrophic disruption produces **500–1500 m/s**. The required velocity depends on the integration time since disruption.

**Statistical modeling of coincidence probability.** A formal statistical test can quantify the probability of two HED falls occurring within 9 days by chance, given the known HED fall rate (~1 per 2–3 years globally). This involves Poisson statistics with the HED fall rate as the rate parameter.
